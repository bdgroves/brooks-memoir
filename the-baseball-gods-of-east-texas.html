The Buccaneers · A Perfect Infield · The Sound of a Bat Catching One

The Baseball Gods of East Texas

Brenham, Texas · Blinn College · 1988–1990 Brooks Groves
Blinn College Stories Texas Brenham 1980s Baseball Coming of Age

One of the Blinn College Stories. Spring 1989. A California kid who could field but never really hit discovers that in a small Texas town, baseball isn't a sport — it's an identity. This one isn't really about baseball. It's about finding out how many different versions of America there are.

When I first arrived at Blinn College in Brenham, Texas, I thought I knew baseball.

After all, I grew up in California. I'd played Little League. I was a first baseman. I could field pretty well. Hitting? Well… it turns out that's a fairly important part of the game.

That was about the extent of my baseball career.

What I didn't know was that baseball could become part of the identity of an entire community.

It took me a while to realize I wasn't just watching Blinn baseball. I was getting my first glimpse into East Texas. Brenham just happened to be my front-row seat.

Every place has its heroes.

In East Texas — at least the little corner of it I got to know during the spring of 1989 — many of those heroes wore white baseball pants dusted with red Texas dirt.

A Completely Different World

The baseball players occupied a completely different world than the rest of us.

The Swedes were probably the most exotic people I'd ever met. Todd was certainly the strangest. Memorial Hall was full of every imaginable personality. Future doctors. Future engineers. Future teachers. Future success stories. Future train wrecks. None of us knew which was which.

Then there were the baseball players.

They weren't just students.

They were the Blinn Buccaneers.

They traveled together. Lived for baseball. Hung out together. Drove shiny pickup trucks and the occasional sports car. They always seemed to have somewhere to be. The prettiest girls on campus certainly seemed to know who they were, and from where I stood they looked like they were already living a completely different college experience than the rest of us.

They might as well have already been professional baseball players.

And the funny thing was… some of them eventually would be.

The Buccaneers Were the Real Thing

I had no idea at eighteen, but I'd wandered into one of the most productive baseball programs in junior-college history. Blinn has been playing since 1937 and has sent 17 players to the major leagues and seen 130 drafted. The biggest name is Don Baylor, who went from Brenham to a nineteen-year big-league career and the 1979 American League MVP award.

The program's foundation was laid by Leroy Dreyer, an NJCAA and American Baseball Coaches Association Hall of Famer who went 653–249 across 27 seasons and took Blinn to four JUCO World Series. The ballpark I used to wander over to is named for him. So the feeling I had — that these guys were operating on some other level — wasn't a kid's exaggeration. It was just true, and I was too new to know why.

I was fascinated.

Not jealous.

Curious.

Why did these eighteen-year-old kids matter so much? Why did the whole town seem to know them?

Growing up in California, I had never seen anything quite like it.

You Could Tell What Mattered by Looking Around

The baseball field was beautiful.

The grass looked perfect. The infield was immaculate. Everything about it said somebody cared deeply about this place.

Meanwhile, our tennis courts had cracks, uneven surfaces, and weeds finding their way up through the pavement. You learned to play around the bad bounces.

Nobody was getting drafted because of the tennis courts.

That wasn't a complaint. It was simply a lesson.

Every place has its priorities.
The Sound

One of my favorite memories was batting practice.

I didn't understand all the strategy. I wasn't studying mechanics. I just loved watching baseballs explode off aluminum bats.

Most swings made that familiar metallic ping.

Then every once in a while there was a different sound.

A sharp, clean ting.

The instant you heard it, everybody knew.

The hitter knew. The pitcher knew. The outfielders knew.

You almost didn't need to watch the baseball.

That sound meant it was going a long way.

Even today, if I hear an aluminum bat catch one just right, my mind doesn't go to California.

It goes straight back to Brenham.

Why the Bats Went Ting

That sound is a genuine artifact of the era. Aluminum bats took over college and amateur baseball in the 1970s because they didn't break, and by the late 1980s the technology had gotten good enough that offensive numbers were becoming faintly ridiculous. The hollow barrel flexes on contact and springs back — the so-called trampoline effect — sending the ball off faster than wood ever could, and ringing as it goes.

The pitch of that ring changes depending on where the ball meets the barrel, which is why a well-struck ball sounds different from every other swing, and why everyone on the field knows before they look. Governing bodies have spent the decades since deliberately detuning aluminum bats for safety, so the ones ringing across that Brenham infield in 1989 were near the peak of the loud years.

They’d Already Chosen

The baseball players would show up to class looking exhausted, like they'd already lived an entire day before most of us had finished our first cup of coffee. Practice. Conditioning. Weights. Games. Road trips. Then somehow they still had to make it through math class.

It was a life I didn't really understand.

Looking back now, I think that's what fascinated me.

Most of us were trying to figure out who we wanted to become.

These guys had already chosen.

Everything they had was pointed toward one dream.

Some afternoons I'd wander over to the ballpark just to watch.

The public address system would crackle across campus on game days, letting everybody know baseball was about to begin. Even if you weren't planning on going, you knew there was a game that afternoon. Baseball wasn't tucked away behind the athletic department. It was woven into the rhythm of campus life.

And I suspect it wasn't just Blinn.

At eighteen I thought this was simply how Brenham worked. Looking back, I think Blinn was just my little window into a much bigger East Texas tradition. If you'd dropped me onto another junior college campus somewhere between Brenham, Tyler, Lufkin, or Nacogdoches, I imagine I would have found the same immaculate ballparks, the same pickup trucks, the same local heroes, and the same young men betting everything they had on one dream.

Then Major League Came Out

One night it seemed like all of Memorial Hall went together.

Just like Catfish Thursday, it became one of those little traditions that made dorm life special.

For two hours none of our little tribes mattered.

The baseball players. The California kid. The computer geeks. The guys who spent way too much time playing Nintendo.

Everybody laughed at the same jokes.

For weeks afterward, all you heard around the dorm was people quoting the movie.

Wild Thing was suddenly everywhere. You couldn't hear that song without somebody pretending they were Ricky Vaughn walking in from the bullpen.

For one brief moment, the entire dorm shared the same soundtrack.

April 7, 1989

The timing was perfect: Major League opened on April 7, 1989 — right in the heart of that spring's baseball season. A movie about a roster of misfits, landing in a dorm full of kids who hadn't figured out yet who they were going to be.

And the dorm wasn't alone in catching it. That same season, a wild young Cubs reliever named Mitch Williams got nicknamed Wild Thing after Charlie Sheen's character, adopted Ricky Vaughn's number 99, and had the Troggs song played over the stadium speakers as he walked to the mound. The whole modern ritual of the closer's entrance music traces back to that film. So when Memorial Hall spent a spring quoting a movie and humming one song, we were doing exactly what every ballpark in America was about to start doing.

Looking back, I don't remember many scores. I couldn't tell you who won on some warm Tuesday afternoon in April.

What I remember are the sounds.

The crackle of the PA drifting across campus. The unmistakable ting of an aluminum bat catching one perfectly. Wild Thing echoing through Memorial Hall.

I remember pickup trucks parked outside the ballpark. I remember a baseball field that looked like somebody loved it.

Most of all, I remember discovering that America is full of little worlds, each with its own heroes.

For one spring in 1989, Brenham, Texas was my classroom.

The baseball players were simply one more lesson.

Not about baseball.

About East Texas.

And about how wonderfully strange and interesting America can be when you’re eighteen years old and curious enough to pay attention.
