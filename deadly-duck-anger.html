Deadly Duck: Anger

The Travel Bug That Came Back · Seattle → Amsterdam → Melbourne · 2001–2026 Brooks Groves
Memoir Geocaching Seattle The Westin Amsterdam Travel 2000s

A true story that starts in a Belltown basement in the autumn of 2001 and ends in an Amsterdam park twenty-five years later. The duck is real. The logs are real, and quoted exactly as they were written. The National Park Service really did confiscate him. Everything here is drawn from a database that has been quietly keeping the receipts since before most of it seemed like it would ever matter.

In July of 2026 I was standing in a park in Amsterdam, checking my phone for nearby caches, when I got the distinct feeling I had done this before.

Not déjà vu exactly. Something more specific than that. The unshakable sense that some part of me had already been standing in this exact park, holding a different device, on a much colder day.

It turned out I was right, though not in the way I expected.

I hadn't left a piece of myself there.

I'd left a very angry rubber duck.

The Westin, Autumn 2001

I worked at the Westin Seattle for twenty-one years as a Bell Captain, and in all that time I can count on one hand the number of occasions the hotel felt genuinely strange.

September of 2001 was one of them.

The towers came down on the 11th and the entire American travel industry simply stopped moving. Flights were grounded, then nobody wanted to fly anyway. The conventions cancelled. The tour groups cancelled. A nine-hundred-room hotel discovered it did not need very many bell staff for people who were not arriving.

I went down to a day or two a week. Eventually I went on unemployment.

What I got in exchange was something I had almost never had during those years — an enormous, unwelcome, wide-open surplus of time.

I had a basement studio at the Arkona in Belltown, a bicycle, and a GPS receiver that needed a minute or two of standing perfectly still before it would admit where on Earth the two of us happened to be.

Geocaching was brand new then. Brand new in the sense that the entire hobby was about sixteen months old.

The Switch That Started It

For most of the GPS era the military deliberately degraded the civilian signal — a policy called Selective Availability. A handheld unit could tell you roughly where you were, give or take the length of a football field, which is fine for a boat and useless for finding a bucket.

At midnight on May 2nd, 2000 — geocachers still call it Blue Switch Day — the government switched it off. Civilian accuracy improved roughly tenfold overnight.

The next day, a computer consultant named Dave Ulmer hid a black plastic bucket in the woods outside Beavercreek, Oregon, posted the coordinates to a Usenet group, and called it a GPS Stash. It is still listed today as GCF — one of the shortest cache codes in existence. Somebody found it. Then somebody else hid one. That was the whole origin — no company, no app, just a policy change and a bucket.

By the fall of 2001 there were a few thousand caches in the entire world. There was no app. There were no statistics worth arguing about. You printed the page out, or you wrote the numbers on a scrap of paper, and you went and looked.

It felt less like a hobby than like membership in a very small secret society that occasionally left strange objects in the woods for one another.

Which was, at that particular moment in my life, exactly what I needed.

The Ride

Nine days after September 11th, I found a cache called Golden Child.

I remember beating around in the brush at dusk, waiting for the GPS to settle, working my way around the target zone and hoping I was not about to wake up something that lived in entirely the wrong tree. I took a sticker and a Where's George dollar and left a Club Cal-Neva coin from Reno and the finest watch anybody ever did see. There was a good sunset. It was a nice way to end the day.

Then I did what we all did back then. I went home and read the logs.

That was the whole ritual. You came in, kicked your shoes off, dialed up, and read what everyone else had written about the same handful of caches. Fifty people in the region, maybe. You knew their handles the way you'd know regulars at a bar.

And then one evening about ten days later, there was something in the Golden Child log that had not been there before.

A new kind of thing. Not a trade item — something with a number stamped on it that you were supposed to move. An object that was going somewhere, and wanted a ride.

I want to tell you I thought about it. I didn't. I got up, hauled the bike up the basement stairs into the daylight, and rode north out of Belltown to go get it, on a weekday afternoon, in a city that had gone very quiet, because I had absolutely nothing else to do and nowhere else to be.

Why that one? Why then?

Who knows.

I certainly didn't. What I did not know, riding out there, was that I was about to become the second person on Earth to hold that particular object — one day after it entered the world, and about a month after the whole idea of it existed at all.

Its name was Deadly Duck: Anger.

TB1A.

Seven Deadly Ducks

The entire trackable system — every travel bug and geocoin that millions of people have moved around the planet for twenty-five years, including the one that eventually made it to the International Space Station — begins with seven rubber ducks.

Jeremy Irish, the co-founder of Geocaching.com, released the first one on August 30th, 2001. He wrote up a story to go with it about travelling north through New Mexico and encountering a vortex, out of which came seven rubber ducks from the gates of hell.

Not Hell the flaming sort, he was careful to specify. The lowercase hell, where all the mischievous critters live.

Envy. Sloth. Pride. Gluttony. Lust. Greed.

And Anger.

The Rules of Engagement

If you wanted to travel with a duck, you had to confess a sin of the appropriate variety in your log. That was the price of carriage.

But only a small one. According to the listing, heavier sins caused the poor ducks to burst into flames — the serious material was to be left to the big flaming ducks from the capital-H version.

Seven ducks went into the world in the autumn of 2001. Anger was TB1A: the first tracking number ever issued, the beginning of the numbering system every trackable since has been counted against.

I have thought about this off and on for twenty-five years and it still makes me laugh. Somewhere in a Seattle apartment in 2001 a man wrote several hundred words of demonology for a bag of rubber ducks he'd bought so people would move them around.

The Portrait

There is an official photograph on the Deadly Duck trackable page, and for twenty-five years I never looked at it properly.

Groundspeak has said the image is photoshopped to replicate the mug shot of a famous Seattle-area entrepreneur and philanthropist, taken during his younger and wilder days. They have never named him.

Look at the placard hanging under the duck. It reads ALBUQUERQUE N MEX. On December 13th, 1977, the Albuquerque police booked a twenty-two-year-old Harvard dropout for running a stop sign in Paul Allen's car without a license, and took a photograph of him grinning that has been circulating ever since. He was born in Seattle. He is, by any measure, a famous entrepreneur and philanthropist.

And Jeremy's origin story has him travelling north through New Mexico when the vortex opened.

The official portrait of my duck is Bill Gates's arrest photo with a rubber duck's head on it. It took me a quarter of a century to notice.

For a duck named Anger, what followed would turn out to be one of the gentlest stories I know.

The Confessions

Here is mine, written the evening of September 30th, 2001:

Logged · September 30, 2001

People that talk on their cell phones, while they are driving, and don't pay attention to you, while you are on your bike! It's their world, and we are just in it? This duck is leaving town. Will he come back? Who knows.

Cell phones. That was my great sin. I confessed to being annoyed by traffic.

Over the following fifteen months, five more adults on three continents were required to unburden themselves to this duck, and here is the complete accounting of the darkness they produced:

People who ride an escalator to the top and then just stand there. Cache pages that don't tell you where to park or whether you need a permit. Geocachers who log finds on caches they were never actually at. Having to do somebody else's job because they won't do it themselves.

And my favorite, from a man in Louisiana who is well known in the hobby for having built the difficulty-rating calculator half the world still uses. He confessed to a lifelong grievance with smokers who unerringly position themselves upwind of him. He noted that had he been thinking clearly he could have filled the duck with water and squirted their cigarettes out — but he was too busy making mean faces behind their backs to remember he was, in fact, holding a glass of water at the time.

He closed by saying he hoped to be rid of the duck soon, before his cat decided that jealousy trumps anger and took a liking to the little guy.

Six people. A hell-duck. An explicit mandate to confess.

Not one real sin among them.

I think that is my favorite part of the whole story. You hand a group of strangers a devil and demand their worst, and what comes out is escalators and parking information. Nobody was actually angry. Nobody has ever really been angry. We're all just standing behind somebody on an escalator.

Amsterdam, January 2002

In January I went to Amsterdam.

I want to be clear about how this trip was financed, because it is the single most 2002 detail in the entire account: I was collecting unemployment as a result of the September 11th attacks, and I used the time to go to Europe.

I do not offer a defense. I was thirty-one, the hotel didn't need me, and it turns out that when the world stops and hands you a season of unstructured time, you either fall apart or you go somewhere.

I took the duck with me.

Amsterdam Urban 1 was one of the earliest caches in Europe — the kind of thing where you genuinely did not know whether anyone had been there before you. Perfect sunny day, cold, one of those low northern winter lights. I always did like getting some quality nature in while on the road.

I left him in the park and wrote:

Logged · Amsterdam Urban 1 · January 2002

This park is great!! Yea, there are ducks in the water, and they seem to be having too much fun. I left the Anger Duck, but don't think the duck fits the mood. Why don't you take it to a volcano!

I still think that's a reasonable instruction. He was a wrath duck. He'd been dropped among a population of genuinely contented Dutch waterfowl, which struck me as an insult to everyone involved. Somebody should have taken him somewhere with geological activity.

Nobody ever did.

The Boomerang

Then something happened that I did not think was possible.

He came back.

A cacher picked him up in Amsterdam a week later — their confession was the escalator people — and mentioned they might be heading home before long. Home, as it turned out, was Washington State.

In February he surfaced in a cache called Disappearing Cache, about seventy miles from where I'd first found him.

There are hundreds of thousands of travel bugs loose in the world. I have held exactly one of the original seven.

And somehow I held him twice.

Logged · Disappearing Cache · February 2002

Bam! I took this thing all the way to Holland, and then it came back to Washington. I found it again, and took it further into the wilds. Pretty cool to think how many miles this duck has traveled. Is it a record?

I asked that question in February of 2002 and nobody answered it. Hold that thought.

Faced with a duck that had crossed the Atlantic, crossed the continent, and returned to me like a stray cat, I concluded there was only one reasonable course of action.

Somebody had to take him somewhere he could not get out of.

The Quietest Place

I drove west, and then I kept driving west, out past Port Angeles and down the long road toward Ozette, which is about as far west as you can go in the lower forty-eight before the country gives up and becomes ocean.

The Man Whose Yard It Was

Peter Roose came from Bollnäs, Sweden, and settled out past Ozette in 1907. He cleared a piece of the rainforest, built a cabin and a barn and a root house out of western redcedar, raised sheep and potatoes and berries, and lived there largely alone until he died in 1944.

His neighbors were a handful of other Scandinavians and the Makah community at Ozette. His homestead is the last of its kind in western Washington — which is why it's still standing, and why it went onto the National Register in 2007.

The cabin is there. The fence posts are there.

The last two tenths of a mile I crawled army-style. No worries — I'm from Yosemite, and I grew up crawling around in the woods like that.

What I was not prepared for was the silence.

I wrote at the time that it was the quietest place I had ever been, which probably says more about a man who lived in downtown Seattle than it does about the Olympic rainforest. But I stood there in a dead man's yard at the edge of the continent and could not hear one single thing.

I left the duck and wrote:

Logged · Roose Homestead · Spring 2002

I figure it will be safe out here for awhile.

Famous last words.

Seized by the Federal Government

Ten weeks later the National Park Service found him.

Let me put that another way, because it deserves the room.

The National Park Service confiscated a devil duck from Olympic National Park and mailed him back to Geocaching headquarters in a padded envelope.

Jeremy's log, dated April 24th, 2002, notes that the good folks at NPS were able to force Anger duck into an envelope to ship back to me, and that he was now hanging around the Groundspeak offices until the Louisiana trip the following week. He added that the duck seemed a bit miffed about being shipped in such a way, but that this was his general attitude anyway.

There are photographs. He is lying on the envelope. He looks exactly as annoyed as you would expect.

Somewhere out there in the spring of 2002 a park ranger stood in an office at the edge of the country, holding a small rubber duck with devil horns, and had to explain to a colleague what they were doing and why postage was involved. I would pay real money for the transcript.

It's also, quietly, a moment of actual history. In 2002 the Park Service was working out its position on people leaving containers in national parks, and the position it arrived at was no. My duck was evidence in that argument. He got caught up in the first jurisdictional fight this hobby ever had.

And then he lived at Groundspeak for eleven days. The wrath duck went home to head office and cooled off.

The Long Way Round

After his detention, Jeremy carried him personally to Louisiana, which is where the smoker business happened.

From there: Georgia, where a man at a geocachers' meeting picked him up and confessed to caring too much about his find count, then explained that getting over it had been a twelve step program, and that he now just enjoyed the hobby and competed only against himself.

I liked that man immediately.

Then Connecticut, carried north on somebody's work trip. Then a short hop into New York, into a cache named for the dog that guards the underworld — nobody planned that, it just happened. Then a couple in New York spotted him, were frightened by his hissing, left him where he was, and then reported that he began appearing in their dreams insisting they keep him moving, until they gave in and drove back for him.

They took him to Australia.

In Melbourne he was retrieved from a hollow tree beside the Yarra River, where he was reported to be extremely frustrated at being so close to the water and unable to reach it, which is a fair complaint from a duck. He spent a week on Port Phillip Bay, requested that his hosts photograph him enjoying the view, wrestled a Dalmatian named Jessie, and won.

There is a photograph of him sitting on the dog's head.

All told he covered something like twenty-three and a half thousand miles.

The website claims 24,011.5.

It's wrong. I checked. I pulled all three pages of his history, laid the legs end to end, and found that the site credits a four-hundred-mile trip from Louisiana to Georgia with more than two thousand miles, and a nine-mile hop across Melbourne with eight thousand. Two screenshots of the same page, taken two hours apart, gave different numbers for the same events.

So: twenty-four years after asking is it a record? on a February evening, I have become enough of a GIS professional to answer my own question, and the answer is that the record is bad data.

Life takes strange turns.

Christmas Day

His last cache was in Royal Park, Melbourne.

On Christmas Day of 2002 the man who had placed him there posted his obituary. The cache had been plundered. The duck was gone. He noted, with what I've always read as genuine affection, that the duck's anger may have played a part in this cache's demise himself, and gained his freedom.

He extended his sympathy to everyone who had crossed the duck's path.

Fifteen months. Three continents. Six confessions. One federal deportation. And then, on Christmas Day, an anger duck escapes captivity during a burglary and is never seen again.

You could not write it. If you put it in a story, they'd cut it.

What Became of the Others

I went looking for his siblings, which I had somehow never thought to do. Three of them turn up. One has a genuine unsolved mystery attached. And Anger, it turns out, lands squarely in the middle of the pack.

The Siblings

Greed — still out there, technically. Jeremy told the story publicly in 2015: on December 16th, 2001 he was visiting a friend in Franklin County, Virginia, and the two of them placed a cache together — an old decoy duck, the Greed devil duck, some trade items, a logbook. A short loop trail, down a hill beside a small pond, in either Waid Park or the county recreation park; they were never sure which. Then the coordinates were lost before he could list it. It has never been published, never been found, and probably never been touched. Groundspeak has openly invited people to go and look.

Envy — TB1. The first trackable number ever issued. Status: Unknown Location. A hundred and six records, fifty-nine people still watching. And here is the strange part: its distance field is blank. The first trackable in history has no recorded mileage at all. Its recent logs aren't finds — they're people who spotted it in a film reel at the 2021 Geocaching International Film Festival and logged a note saying seen.

Gluttony — TB18B, released October 8th, 2001. Eleven thousand eight hundred and ninety-eight miles, three hundred records, thirty-eight gallery images. He survived, went home, and is currently on display at Geocaching HQ in Seattle. Visitors log discoveries on the tour constantly; the most recent was two weeks ago. One of them, this past May, confessed to ordering ice cream after an enormous meal — twenty-four years after the mission text asked for exactly that.

Sloth, Pride and Lust I could not pull clean records for. The search keeps hitting the replica tags Groundspeak sold for the fifteenth anniversary, which crowd out the originals entirely.

So the accounting comes out like this. One duck went home to a display case in Seattle and gets greeted by strangers on a guided tour. One is lying in the leaves somewhere in Virginia, unlisted and unfound, and has been for twenty-five years. One has no mileage at all and exists now only as something people glimpse in a film and log a single word about.

And mine out-travelled the one in the display case by roughly double — about twenty-three and a half thousand miles to Gluttony's eleven thousand nine hundred — and got nothing for it but a burglary in Melbourne on Christmas Day.

I find I don't mind that at all. The one in the case is safe and the one in Virginia is undisturbed, but neither of them is still going anywhere.

Amsterdam, July 2026

The duck has been gone for twenty-three years.

People still log him. Somebody in 2023 turned up his tracking code by accident and left an apologetic note. This past February, someone logged a single word.

Nähty. Finnish. It means: seen.

Which brings me back to that park.

I was in Amsterdam in July for Aimee's fiftieth — nine days, Amsterdam and London, the sort of trip you plan for a year. And on one of those afternoons I stood in a park with a phone in my hand, feeling like I'd stood there before, and eventually went and looked it up.

I had. January of 2002. Same park. Different device. Different century, more or less. A younger man on unemployment with a duck in his coat pocket, who had no idea what any of it was going to turn into, and no particular reason to be there either.

The older I get, the more I think travel has less to do with the places than with running into whoever you were the last time you visited them.

I keep coming back to how thin the whole thing is.

If the towers don't come down, I'm working. If I'm working, there's no afternoon free. If there's no free afternoon, I don't read the logs, don't get on the bike, don't ride north, and somebody else — probably somebody perfectly nice — picks up TB1A on October the second and this is their story instead of mine. If a stranger in Amsterdam hadn't happened to live in Washington, he never comes back and I only hold him once. If a park ranger hadn't gone out to Ozette that spring with a mail run to do, he's still lying in the grass at a dead Swede's homestead.

Remove any single one of those and none of it happens.

Which is, I think, the actual point, and it took me twenty-five years to see it:

Travel bugs don't travel. People do.

They get carried by strangers who were going somewhere else anyway, for reasons none of them could adequately explain to one another. A work trip. A vacation nobody could afford. A ranger with paperwork. A burglar in Melbourne on Christmas Day. A guy on a bicycle who had nothing else to do that afternoon and never could say why he went.

The duck disappeared a long time ago.

The story didn't.

Somewhere in a database neither of us could have imagined in the fall of 2001, he is still, technically, travelling.

And every once in a while, someone still finds him.

Nähty.

Seen.
